Limits of this review

  • It is not a systematic review. We selected 30 studies for relevance, then graded them.
  • It includes vendor studies from both sides of the synthetic-respondent debate. Each card flags the vendor's commercial interest.
  • It is not a forecast. AI models change quickly. Each card shows the period or model tested.
Why I created this site

A researcher's way through the AI noise

I am a researcher. For about 15 years, I have worked in audience research, measurement and analytics. I founded Zeta Impact Advisory, a research and measurement practice. Before that, I was a senior program officer for measurement and insights at the Gates Foundation. Earlier, I worked across technology, media and telecommunications

AI has made many knowledge workers anxious. Market and consumer research is one of the fields most affected, and it is my field. Every week brings new claims. Some say AI will replace surveys and interviews. Others say AI research is worthless. Both kinds of claim are loud. Few of them show their evidence.

A large, vague worry is hard to manage. It becomes smaller when you divide it into specific questions and check the evidence for each one.

This is a personal project. I built it to learn how my industry, and the areas I have worked in, are changing with AI. I also built it for people who are working through the same questions.

Collection 1 · AI in Market and Consumer Research · 15 studies

Can I trust AI to help me understand my consumers or audineces?

Assessment The answer depends on the level of detail you need. AI does well when it codes text into defined categories. It does reasonably well when it ranks options. It is less reliable for whole-population estimates. It is not reliable for the spread of opinion or for subgroups but many decisions depend on subgroups.

Five levels of detail · Each level asks more of the AI · Verdicts are our assessment
01

Code text into categories

Open-ended survey answers, annotation, interview coding with a codebook

Use

AI was more accurate than crowd workers on defined categories. Agreement with human experts was moderate for interpretive codes. Check a human-coded sample.Gilardi 2023 · Zhang 2025 · Arora 2025

02

Rank options

Which message, concept or treatment is stronger

Use

AI-predicted effects correlated with actual effects (r = 0.85, preprint figure). The AI overestimated effect sizes. Use AI to shorten a list. Test the finalists with real people.Ashokkumar/Hewitt 2026 · Maier 2025 · Li 2024

03

Estimate whole-population results

What share of adults think or do something

Validate first

Error was about 3 points on common political questions and about 20 points on coffee questions. Some willingness-to-pay estimates had the wrong sign.Verasight 2025–26 · Bisbee 2024 · Brand 2025

04

Estimate the spread of opinion

Variance, intensity, minority views

Not yet

Synthetic answers had less variance than real answers. With answer order randomized, answers moved toward random. One new method narrowed the gap in one product category.Bisbee 2024 · Dominguez-Olmedo 2024 · Wang 2025 · Maier 2025

05

Estimate subgroups and relationships

Crosstabs, segment sizes, driver and regression analysis

Not yet

Error increased as groups got smaller. Models missed the same groups again and again, for example older and religious people and non-Western populations. Regression results differed from real data.Verasight · Santurkar 2023 · Atari 2023 · Bisbee 2024 · Wang 2025

Source finding · Error increases as groups get smaller

Mean absolute error between synthetic respondents and a real survey, in percentage points. Source: Verasight Synthetic Sampling Reports I, III and IV (2025–26). “≈10” is the average subgroup error stated in the reports. “Up to 30” is the largest subgroup error they report. It is not an average. The publisher sells human survey panels.

Assessment · firm

Synthetic respondents are not yet reliable for subgroup analysis

We tested this position against the 15 studies. It holds.

Evidence for this verdict

  • Topline results can match while the structure under them fails. Variance and regression results differed from real data (Bisbee 2024).
  • Error increased as groups got smaller (Verasight).
  • Models missed the same groups across tests (Santurkar 2023; Atari 2023).
  • Models showed groups as outsiders see them, and removed variation within groups (Wang 2025).

Strongest evidence against. Park et al. (2024) built simulated respondents from two-hour interviews with 1,052 real people. This reduced accuracy gaps between racial and ideological groups. But each simulated respondent depends on a real person's interview. The method copies people you already studied. It does not replace a sample.

What would change this verdict. Independent, preregistered tests that meet all three conditions:

  1. The questions were asked after the model's training cutoff.
  2. Subgroup estimates fall within normal sampling error of a probability sample.
  3. The tests cover several topics or product categories, including small groups.
Recommendation For insights teams · Based on the studies above

Use now

  • Code open-ended answers and transcripts with AI. Have a person code a sample of each wave. Compare the two results.
  • Draft discussion guides, screeners and message variants.
  • Screen message or concept variants. Then test the shortlist with real people.

Validate first

  • Whole-population estimates. First compare AI results with your past survey results in the same category.
  • Concept ranking from text-based answers. First compare it with your past concept tests.
  • Models fine-tuned on your own survey history.

Not yet

  • Any result for a segment, demographic group or crosstab cell.
  • Driver analysis, regression and segment sizing.
  • New categories, new products, non-Western markets and hard-to-reach groups.
Open questions
  • Do newer models reduce subgroup error? Most subgroup tests in this review used models from 2022 to 2025.
  • Does the text-based rating method (Maier 2025) work outside personal care? Does it work for subgroups?
  • How much of your own survey data does a fine-tuned model need before its toplines are reliable?

Collection 2 · Reactions to AI Disclosure · 15 studies

How will my audience respond when they know AI was involved?

Assessment Disclosure reduced trust in almost every controlled study in this review. In both field experiments, it also reduced purchases or clicks. The size of the drop depends on the message and on what the audience assumes AI did. Marketing communications teams can control both.

Assessment How disclosure affects response · Our model, built from the studies
01 · Signal

The audience learns that AI was involved

The source can be the sender, a platform label or a third party. Exposure by a third party caused the largest drop (Schilke 2025).

02 · Assumption

The audience estimates how much AI did

Without details, people assumed AI did all the work (Altay 2024).

03 · Judgement

The audience rates authenticity and effort

Emotional messages lost the most (Kirk 2025; Rubin 2025).

04 · Behavior

Belief, sharing, clicks and purchases change

Stated intentions changed little. Observed clicks and purchases changed a lot (Wittenberg 2025; Luo 2019; Lee 2025).

Recommendation

Disclose. Say specifically what AI did. Do not depend on “human oversight” wording.

Hiding AI use is a risk. In one set of experiments, exposure by a third party reduced trust more than self-disclosure did (Schilke 2025).

A specific statement works better than a general label. People assumed full automation when a label gave no detail (Altay 2024). In one news study, a list of sources removed the trust drop (Toff 2025).

Human leadership helped more than human oversight. Comfort was 21% for news made mostly by AI with human oversight. It was 43% for news made mostly by people with AI help (Reuters 2025). Advice from “a physician with AI” was still rated less reliable than advice from a physician alone (Reis 2024). If a person led the work, say so. If AI led the work, oversight wording will probably not restore trust.

Recommendation For marketing communications teams

  1. State the task AI did. For example: “Written by our team. Edited with AI.” Do not write only “AI-assisted.”
  2. Show sources on informational content.
  3. Use a human author for thank-you notes to donors, condolences, apologies and leadership messages.
  4. Test label words in each market. Many people did not understand “synthetic” or “edited” as labels, and understanding differed by country (Epstein 2023).
  5. Measure behavior in A/B tests, for example clicks, conversions and donations. Do not depend on stated intentions.
  6. Watch your most loyal audience. People with high trust in news and more knowledge of journalism showed the largest drop (Toff 2025).
Open questions
  • Does the penalty get smaller as AI use becomes normal? One 2019 study suggests it can (Jakesch). No long-term field study tests this.
  • Most studies measure one exposure. The effect on repeat customers and long-term donors is not known.
  • The two field studies cover one financial-services firm and one beauty retailer. The effect for nonprofits and other categories is not known.

Industry history · Market and consumer research

Has research been through a change like this before?

Assessment Yes, twice. The telephone and then the internet each made research cheaper and faster. Each time, methodologists warned about a specific loss of quality. Each time, most of the work moved to the new method anyway. The old method did not disappear. It moved to the work that needs its quality. AI looks like a third change of this kind, with one important difference: AI can generate the answers, not only collect them.

FrameworkQuestion it answersSource
Three eras of survey researchWhat changed in how researchers selected and reached people?Groves, Public Opinion Quarterly, 2011
Total survey errorWhich kind of error did each new method add or reduce? The framework splits error into coverage, sampling, nonresponse and measurement error.Groves & Lyberg, Public Opinion Quarterly, 2010
Disruptive innovationWhy does a cheaper, lower-quality method take most of the market? Disrupters start with customers who need only a “good enough” product, then improve and move up.Christensen, Raynor & McDonald, Harvard Business Review, 2015
Task-based model of automationWhat happens to the work? Automation removes tasks from people (displacement). New technology also creates new tasks for people (reinstatement).Acemoglu & Restrepo, Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2019

Why these four: the first two come from survey methodology and describe this industry directly. The last two are established, widely tested theories of markets and work. We do not use a generic “technology adoption arc,” because no single arc is an established law.

Source findings Three eras, and an emerging fourth
EraHow researchers selected and reached peopleWhat became cheaper or fasterMain quality riskWhat happened to the old method
1 · Invention
1930–1960		 Area and quota samples. Face-to-face and mail interviews. Scientific sampling replaced very large straw polls. In 1936 the Literary Digest received 2.4 million ballots and predicted the wrong winner. Gallup used a much smaller sample and predicted the right one. Sample design, not sample size. Quota samples also failed in 1948, and probability sampling replaced them. Straw polls lost credibility. The Literary Digest went bankrupt.
2 · Expansion
1960–1990s		 Random-digit dialing. Telephone interviews, later computer-assisted (CATI). Telephone became an accepted method in the 1970s, when about 90% of US households had a telephone. It then passed face-to-face interviewing as the most common method. Coverage at first (homes with no phone). Later nonresponse: Pew's telephone response rate fell from 36% in 1997 to 6% in 2018. Face-to-face interviewing continued, but it became the less common method.
3 · Online
1990s–2020s		 Opt-in online panels. Self-completed web surveys. Online research grew fast. About 56% of national US polls before the 2016 election used online methods; in 2020 the share was 84%. Coverage and selection. In 2010, AAPOR advised researchers not to use opt-in panels to estimate population values. Pew later found that opt-in samples had about twice the error of probability-based panels. Live phone became the only method for 10% of national pollsters in 2022. Probability-based online panels grew: from 7 to 23 national pollsters between 2016 and 2022.
4 · AI
2023– · Emerging		 AI across the workflow: survey design, AI-moderated interviews, AI coding, synthetic respondents, research agents. Speed and scale. Vendors report that AI moderators can run hundreds of interviews in a day. Vendor surveys report fast adoption. A new kind of error. Synthetic respondents have no sampling frame. Their error comes from the model. The total survey error framework does not yet name it. Too early to know. See the predictions below.

Sources: Groves 2011; course and encyclopedia summaries of the 1936 and 1948 polls; SAGE Encyclopedia of Survey Research Methods; Pew Research Center 2012, 2019 and 2023; AAPOR Report on Online Panels 2010. Full list at the end of this page. Era dates follow Groves (2011). Some authors date the end of the second era at 2000.

Assessment The pattern across the first three eras
01 · Cheaper

A new method lowers cost or time

Telephone replaced travel. Online replaced interviewers.

02 · Warning

Experts name a specific risk

Phone: coverage, then nonresponse. Online: selection bias (AAPOR 2010).

03 · Foothold

Volume moves anyway

It starts with work where “good enough” is enough. This matches disruptive innovation.

04 · Repair

The field builds new methods

Efficient random-digit dialing (1978). Weighting. Probability-based online panels.

05 · Niche

The old method stays for high-stakes work

It becomes the benchmark that checks the new method.

What history cannot tell us: whether total employment in research will grow or shrink. In the task-based model, the net effect depends on the balance between displacement and new tasks. History shows both forces, not which one will be larger this time.

Open questions Where this time may be different

  1. AI can generate the respondent. Telephone and online changed how researchers reached real people. Synthetic respondents remove the person. No earlier change did this.
  2. Errors may stay hidden longer. Election polls are tested in public on election day. Most market research has no public test. A wrong synthetic result can affect decisions for a long time before anyone finds it.
  3. Adoption data is weak. The fastest adoption figures come from vendor surveys of stated use and expectations. They are not measures of actual project volume.

Source findings How fast the fourth era is moving

  • In a 2024 Qualtrics survey of about 3,000 researchers, 71% agreed that most market research will use synthetic responses within three years. Vendor survey · stated expectations
  • In Qualtrics' 2025 survey, 78% predicted that AI agents will run more than half of research projects by 2028. Vendor survey · stated expectations
  • In blind tests, expert moderators rated standard AI moderators poorly on rapport, improvisation and emotional cues. Human moderators still found more unexpected insights. Vendor tests · conference paper

For comparison: in 2010 the AAPOR task force recommended against using opt-in panels to estimate population values. A decade later, most national polls were online. Expert warnings did not stop the shift. They shaped the methods that made it work.

Method

How we select, grade and summarize studies

We use one rubric for both collections. If you disagree with a verdict, you can see which studies it depends on.

Selection

  • AI in Audience Research: studies that compare AI output with data from real people.
  • Reactions to AI Disclosure: studies that randomly vary whether people believe AI was involved.
  • We prefer peer-reviewed studies, large or preregistered samples, and settings close to audience research and marketing communications.
  • We include some preprints and industry reports when they test a common use case. Their grades show their limits.
  • Version 1 has 30 studies, 15 in each collection.

Why we do not average the figures

A median across studies is meaningful only when the studies use the same measure. These studies do not. Disclosure studies measure purchase rates, click-through rates, trust scales and belief ratings. Synthetic-respondent studies measure correlations, mean absolute error, agreement scores and distribution similarity. An average across these measures would look precise but would have no meaning. We show each figure with its measure. Verdicts depend on the direction and consistency of results across study designs.

From grades to verdicts

  • Use Two or more A or B studies agree. A routine check can find the errors.
  • Validate first Results depend on the topic, the method or your past data. Use only after you compare with your own human data.
  • Not yet A or B studies show systematic errors that a routine check would not find.

How we checked figures

  • We checked each figure against the study's abstract, the publisher's page or the authors' report.
  • For some figures we found only news coverage or a press release. The study card says so.
  • Where a venue or year is not confirmed by the linked source, the study card says so.

Known limits

  • Most studies use English-language, US or Western samples.
  • Models change faster than studies are published. A failure in 2023 may not hold for 2026 models. A success in one category may not hold in yours.
  • Most disclosure studies measure first reactions. Long-term effects are mostly untested.
  • Study selection and verdicts involve judgement.
