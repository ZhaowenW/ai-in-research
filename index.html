A review of how AI shapes market research, marketing communications, and more

What does the evidence actually say about AI and audiences?

This site reviews the published research on AI and audiences for insights and marketing communications leaders. It doesn't run new studies. Instead, it reads the ones that exist, grades how much weight each can bear and translates the findings into positions you can act on, organized around three questions.

Collection 1 · AI in Audience Research · 26 studies

AI can read your audience's words. It can't yet stand in for your audience.

Key question: Can I trust AI to help me understand my audience?

Our take

How much you can trust AI depends on how much detail you need. It performs well when the job is coding text into defined categories, and reasonably well when the job is ranking options against one another. It becomes unreliable for whole-population estimates outside heavily polled topics, and it is not yet reliable for the spread of opinion or for subgroups, which is where most audience decisions are actually made.

The newer studies in this collection sharpen that picture rather than change it. Models don't react to question wording the way people do, their errors shift unpredictably from topic to topic, and the same subgroup problems show up outside the US.

Key takeaways

  • Coding is the safest use. AI beat crowd workers on defined categories and applied an existing codebook far more reliably than it invented a new one, though accuracy still varied from task to task.
  • Rankings travel; magnitudes don't. AI-simulated experiments ranked real effects well but overstated their size.
  • Averages hide the problem. Synthetic toplines can land close while variance, relationships and subgroups miss badly.
  • Your own data is the multiplier. Models fine-tuned on real answers to the same questions performed far better than models asked to imagine new ones.
Five levels of detail · Each asks more of the AI · Verdicts are our assessment
01

Collect and code open text

Open-ended survey answers, annotation, codebook-based interview coding, AI-moderated interviews

Use

AI was more accurate than crowd workers on defined categories, came within a point of a second human coder on survey open-ends and applied human codebooks with high agreement, but it was much weaker at inventing its own codes, and accuracy varied sharply from task to task. AI interviewers collected rich data for structured topics while lagging expert moderators on rapport and unexpected insight. Check a human-coded sample either way.Gilardi 2023 · Törnberg 2023 · Mellon 2024 · Ziems 2024 · Pangakis 2023 · Jennings 2026 · Zhang 2025 · Wuttke 2025 · Chopra 2023 · Geiecke 2024 · Ipsos 2025 · Arora 2025

02

Rank options

Which message, concept or treatment is stronger

Use

AI-predicted effects tracked actual effects closely (r = 0.85, preprint figure) and replicated the direction of several classic experiments, though they overstated effect sizes, and in simulated tests a change to one variable can quietly shift others. Use AI to shorten a long list, then test the finalists with real people.Ashokkumar/Hewitt 2026 · Aher 2023 · Horton 2023 · Gui 2023 · Maier 2025 · Li 2024

03

Estimate whole-population results

What share of adults think or do something

Validate first

Error ran about 3 points on heavily polled political questions but about 20 points on coffee questions. Willingness-to-pay and patience estimates were often off, sometimes in the wrong direction, and models didn't react to question wording the way people do, while arbitrary prompt layout shifted their answers. Results improved markedly when models were given issue-specific survey data or fine-tuned on real answers to the same questions.Verasight 2025–26 · Bisbee 2024 · Sanders 2023 · Lee 2024 · Brand 2025 · Goli 2024 · Tjuatja 2024 · Brucks 2025 · Kim & Lee 2024

04

Estimate the spread of opinion

Variance, intensity, minority views, knowledge and awareness

Not yet

Synthetic answers were consistently compressed, clustering around a few responses, drifting toward random once answer order was shuffled, or showing implausibly perfect knowledge; even digital twins built from 500 answers per person were less spread out than real people on 94% of outcomes. One new rating method narrowed the gap in a single product category.Bisbee 2024 · Boelaert 2025 · Dominguez-Olmedo 2024 · Li, Li & Qiu 2025 · Toubia 2025 · Aher 2023 · Wang 2025 · Maier 2025

05

Estimate subgroups and relationships

Crosstabs, segment sizes, driver and regression analysis

Not yet

Error grew as groups got smaller, the same groups were missed again and again (younger, less educated, lower-income and minority respondents among them), regression results diverged from real data, and simulation worked best for the US and worst outside Western, English-speaking countries.Verasight · Santurkar 2023 · Bisbee 2024 · Wang 2025 · Atari 2023 · Durmus 2023 · Sanders 2023 · Lee 2024 · Qu & Wang 2024 · Qi 2024 · von der Heyde 2025 · Boelaert 2025 · Toubia 2025

Assessment · firm

Synthetic respondents are not yet reliable for subgroup analysis

We set out to test this position against the evidence rather than simply restate it, and the newer studies make it stronger, not weaker.

Why we hold it

  • Toplines can match while the structure underneath fails, with variance and regression results diverging from real data (Bisbee 2024).
  • Error grows as groups shrink (Verasight), and the same groups are missed across models and countries (Santurkar 2023; Atari 2023; von der Heyde 2025).
  • Models voice outsiders' views of a group and flatten the variety within it (Wang 2025), and their biases shift unpredictably from topic to topic, so reweighting can't fix them (Boelaert 2025).
  • Even digital twins built from about 500 answers per person missed population means, compressed variation and simulated less educated, lower-income and politically extreme people worst (Toubia 2025).

The strongest case against. Park et al. (2024) built simulated respondents from two-hour interviews with 1,052 real people and narrowed accuracy gaps between racial and ideological groups. Because each agent depends on a real person's interview, though, the method copies people you've already studied; it doesn't replace a sample.

What would change our mind. Independent, preregistered tests that meet all three of these conditions:

  1. The questions were asked after the model's training cutoff.
  2. Subgroup estimates fall within normal sampling error of a probability sample.
  3. The tests span several topics or product categories and include small groups.
Recommendation What it means for insights teams

Use now

  • Code open-ended answers and transcripts with AI, have a person code a sample of each wave, and compare the two.
  • Give AI your codebook rather than asking it to invent one, and validate each new codeframe separately.
  • Draft discussion guides, screeners and message variants, and use AI to screen variants before testing the shortlist with people.

Validate first

  • Whole-population estimates, benchmarked against your past survey results in the same category.
  • AI-moderated interviews for structured topics, with human moderators reviewing a sample of sessions.
  • Models fine-tuned on your own survey history, tested on questions they haven't seen.

Not yet

  • Any number reported for a segment, demographic group or crosstab cell.
  • Questionnaire pretesting on synthetic respondents, since they don't react to wording the way people do and shift with arbitrary prompt layout.
  • Driver analysis, segment sizing, awareness measures, new categories and non-Western markets.
Open questions
  • Do newer models close the subgroup gap? Most subgroup tests in this review used models released between 2022 and 2025.
  • Does the text-based rating method (Maier 2025) hold up outside personal care, and at the subgroup level?
  • How much of your own survey data does a fine-tuned model need before its toplines can be trusted on new questions?
  • Can AI moderators match expert humans on depth for emotionally complex topics, or only for structured ones?

Collection 2 · Reactions to AI Disclosure · 26 studies

Disclosure usually costs trust, but how you disclose decides how much.

Key question: How will my audience respond when they know AI was involved?

Our take

Telling people AI was involved lowered trust in nearly every controlled study we reviewed, and in both large field experiments it also cut clicks or purchases. Audiences want to know, though: three-quarters of US adults say it matters to them, and being exposed by someone else costs more than disclosing.

The size of the penalty is far from fixed. It is largest for emotional messages, creative work and luxury brands, and when people assume AI did everything. It shrinks when the disclosure is specific, when people remain visibly in charge, and when AI handles the setting rather than the substance. Some of it, one study suggests, is less an aversion to AI than a bonus audiences give to human effort.

The penalty is also narrower than it first appears. It lands mainly on trust in the sender and on how accurate the content seems; in two large experiments, including one with a UK probability sample, an AI label didn't make a message less persuasive or change people's policy views.

Key takeaways

  • Silence is the riskier bet. Third-party exposure cut trust more than self-disclosure.
  • Specific beats generic. A bare “AI-generated” label invites people to assume full automation.
  • Leadership beats oversight. “Human-led with AI help” earned twice the comfort of “AI-led with human oversight.”
  • Surveys understate the cost. Stated intentions barely moved while real clicks and purchases fell sharply.
  • The sender pays more than the message. Labels cut trust in the creator, yet labeled messages persuaded as well as unlabeled ones.
Assessment How disclosure affects response · Our model, built from the studies
01 · Signal

The audience learns AI was involved

The signal can come from the sender, a platform label or a third party, and exposure by a third party does the most damage (Schilke 2025).

02 · Assumption

They estimate how much AI did

Without details, people assume AI did all the work (Altay 2024), and suspicion alone can trigger the penalty (Hohenstein 2023).

03 · Judgement

They reassess authenticity and effort

Emotional, creative and luxury content loses the most because effort is part of what people value (Kirk 2025; Rubin 2025; To 2025).

04 · Behavior

Belief, sharing, clicks and purchases shift

Stated intentions changed little in surveys, but observed clicks, purchases and cooperation fell sharply when behavior was measured (Wittenberg 2025; Luo 2019; Lee 2025; Ishowo-Oloko 2019).

Recommendation

Disclose, be specific and keep people visibly in charge.

Staying quiet is the riskier strategy. In one set of 13 experiments, being exposed by a third party cut trust further than disclosing did (Schilke 2025), and three-quarters of US adults say knowing matters to them (Pew 2025).

How you disclose matters more than whether you do. People assume full automation when a label gives no detail (Altay 2024), while a list of sources erased the trust gap in one news study (Toff 2025). In advertising, keeping the people and the promise real while using AI for the setting restored trust (Grigsby 2025).

Finally, emphasize human leadership rather than human oversight. Comfort reached 43% for news made mostly by people with AI help, versus 21% for AI-led news with human oversight (Reuters 2025), and advice from “a physician with AI” was still rated less reliable than advice from a physician alone (Reis 2024). If people led the work, say so plainly; if AI did, oversight language probably won't win back the trust.

Recommendation What it means for marketing communications teams

  1. Name the task AI did. “Written by our team, edited with AI” works better than a bare “AI-assisted.”
  2. Show your sources on informational content.
  3. Keep a human author for donor thank-yous, fundraising appeals, condolences, apologies and leadership messages, where the penalty is largest.
  4. Use AI for the scene, not the substance in ads: real people and real claims, AI-generated settings.
  5. Raise the creative bar for premium and luxury brands, since visible effort offsets much of the penalty.
  6. Test label wording in each market, because terms like “synthetic” and “edited” are widely misunderstood.
  7. Measure behavior in A/B tests (clicks, conversions, donations) rather than relying on stated intentions.
Open questions
  • Will the penalty fade as AI use becomes normal? One 2019 study suggests it can, but no long-term field study has tested it.
  • Most studies measure a single exposure, so the effect on repeat customers and long-term donors is unknown.
  • The field evidence comes from one financial-services firm, one beauty retailer and one luxury email test. How the penalty plays out for nonprofits in the field is untested.
  • How much of the penalty is aversion to AI, and how much is a premium for human effort? The answer changes what to emphasize.
  • Why do labels cut trust in the sender but not the persuasive power of the message? Whether that holds for high-salience topics and for brands, not just policy arguments, is untested.

Industry history · Market and consumer research

Research has been disrupted twice before, and the pattern is instructive.

Key question: Has research been through a change like this before?

Our take

Yes, twice. The telephone and then the internet each made research cheaper and faster, and each time methodologists warned, correctly, about a specific loss of quality. Most of the work moved to the new method anyway, while the old method survived as the benchmark for work that needed its quality. AI looks like a third change of this kind, with one important difference: earlier technologies changed how researchers reached people, while AI can generate the answers itself.

Source findings Three eras, and an emerging fourth
EraHow researchers selected and reached peopleWhat became cheaper or fasterMain quality riskWhat happened to the old method
1 · Invention
1930–1960		 Area and quota samples, interviewed face to face or by mail. Scientific sampling replaced giant straw polls. In 1936 the Literary Digest collected 2.4 million ballots and called the election wrong, while Gallup's far smaller sample called it right. Sample design, not sample size. Quota samples failed in turn in 1948, and probability sampling replaced them. Straw polls lost credibility, and the Literary Digest went bankrupt.
2 · Expansion
1960–1990s		 Random-digit dialing, with telephone interviews that later became computer-assisted (CATI). Telephone became an accepted method in the 1970s, once about 90% of US households had a phone, and went on to overtake face-to-face interviewing. Coverage at first, because some homes had no phone. Later nonresponse: Pew's telephone response rate fell from 36% in 1997 to 6% in 2018. Face-to-face interviewing continued, but as the less common method.
3 · Online
1990s–2020s		 Opt-in online panels and self-completed web surveys. Online research grew rapidly. About 56% of national US polls before the 2016 election used online methods; by 2020 the share was 84%. Coverage and selection. In 2010, AAPOR advised against using opt-in panels to estimate population values, and Pew later found opt-in samples had roughly twice the error of probability-based panels. By 2022 live phone was the only method for just 10% of national pollsters, while probability-based online panels grew from 7 to 23 pollsters between 2016 and 2022.
4 · AI
2023– · Emerging		 AI across the workflow: survey design, AI-moderated interviews, AI coding, synthetic respondents and research agents. Speed and scale. Vendors report that AI moderators can run hundreds of interviews in a day, and vendor surveys report rapid adoption. A new kind of error. Synthetic respondents have no sampling frame; their error comes from the model, which the total survey error framework doesn't yet name. Too early to know. See the predictions below.

What history can't tell us: whether total employment in research will grow or shrink. Under the task-based model, the net effect depends on the balance between tasks displaced and tasks created, and history shows both forces at work without telling us which will prove larger this time.

Open questions Where this time may be different

  1. AI can generate the respondent. The telephone and the internet changed how researchers reached real people; synthetic respondents remove the person altogether, which no earlier change did.
  2. Errors may stay hidden longer. Election polls face a public test on election day, but most market research never does, so a wrong synthetic result can steer decisions for a long time before anyone notices.
  3. Adoption data is weak. The fastest adoption figures come from vendor surveys of stated use and expectations, not from measures of actual project volume.

Source findings How fast the fourth era is moving

  • In a 2024 Qualtrics survey of about 3,000 researchers, 71% agreed that most market research will use synthetic responses within three years. Vendor survey · stated expectations
  • In Qualtrics' 2025 survey, 78% predicted that AI agents will run more than half of research projects by 2028. Vendor survey · stated expectations
  • In blind tests, expert moderators rated standard AI moderators poorly on rapport, improvisation and emotional cues, and human moderators still found more unexpected insights. Vendor tests · conference paper

For perspective: in 2010 the AAPOR task force recommended against using opt-in panels for population estimates, and a decade later most national polls were online. Expert warnings didn't stop the shift; they shaped the methods that eventually made it work.

Method

How we select, grade and summarize the studies

A single rubric governs both collections, so a reader who disagrees with a verdict can see exactly which studies it rests on and how each was weighed.

Selection

  • AI in Audience Research includes studies that compare AI output with data from real people on tasks used in audience, market, consumer or opinion research.
  • Reactions to AI Disclosure includes studies that randomly vary whether people believe AI was involved, plus large representative surveys of attitudes toward AI-made content.
  • We include research from AI developers such as Anthropic, Google and Microsoft, and from research vendors and platforms such as Qualtrics, Kantar, Ipsos, Getty Images and Adobe, because they often have data no one else has. Each of these studies carries a “Potential bias” note naming the commercial interest, is graded lower on independence and never supports a verdict on its own. Use the Authors filter in the Study library to see them separately.
  • We favor peer-reviewed studies, large or preregistered samples and settings close to audience research and marketing communications, while including influential preprints and industry reports when they test a common use case. Their grades reflect their limits.
  • This version covers 52 studies: 26 in AI in Audience Research and 26 in Reactions to AI Disclosure.

Why we don't average the figures

A median across studies only means something when the studies share a measure, and these don't. Disclosure studies measure purchase rates, click-through rates, trust scales and belief ratings, while synthetic-respondent studies measure correlations, mean absolute error, agreement scores and distributional similarity. Averaging across them would produce a number that looks precise but means nothing. Instead, we show each figure with its own measure and base verdicts on how consistently results point the same way across different study designs.

What this review is not

  • It isn't a systematic review. We selected studies for their relevance to audience research and marketing communications, then graded them.
  • It includes vendor studies from both sides of the synthetic-respondent debate, and flags each vendor's commercial interest.
  • It isn't a forecast. Models change quickly, so each study card notes the period or model tested.

CurrencyRecent models, or tests on data from after the model's training cutoffOlder models or a pre-2022 setting (noted, but not disqualifying)
AStrong. Rigorous design, adequate scale, independent authors and peer review. Can support a verdict on its own.
BModerate. Sound but narrow, relying on one setting or stated measures, or not yet peer-reviewed. Supports a verdict alongside other studies.
CLimited. A single dataset, an older setting or a commercial interest in the result. A signal rather than proof.

From grades to verdicts

  • Use Two or more A or B studies agree, and any errors can be caught with a routine check.
  • Validate first Results depend on the topic, the method or your own past data, so they're usable only after comparison with your own human data.
  • Not yet A or B studies show systematic errors that a routine check wouldn't catch.

How we checked the figures

  • Each figure was checked against the study's abstract, the publisher's page or the authors' report.
  • Where we could find a figure only in news coverage or a press release, the study card says so, and the same applies to venues or years we couldn't confirm.

Known limits

  • Most studies use English-language, US or Western samples.
  • Models change faster than studies get published, so a failure in 2023 may not hold for 2026 models, and a success in one category may not hold in yours.
  • Most disclosure studies measure first reactions; long-term effects remain largely untested.
  • Selecting studies and setting verdicts both involve judgement.
About

A researcher's way through the AI noise

If you work in research or marketing, you've probably felt the pressure. Every week someone says AI will replace surveys and interviews, and someone else says AI-generated research is worthless. Both claims are loud, and few come with evidence.

Proof and Wonder reviews the published research so you can see what the evidence actually shows. It takes one large, vague worry and breaks it into questions you can check:

  • Which research tasks can AI do well today?
  • How do audiences react when they learn AI was involved?
  • What happened the last time technology reshaped the research industry?

Each study gets a grade for how much weight it can carry. Each use case gets a verdict. Each verdict names the evidence that would change it. The site will cover more topics over time, and the method will stay the same.
